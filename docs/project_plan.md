# Project Plan: Fair Speech Recognition Without Accent Labels

**Aiden Aquino and Josh Fuentes**, CS 445: Machine Learning, Fall 2026

*Markdown copy of the submitted plan. The submitted version (LaTeX, together with the annotated bibliography) is in Overleaf.*

**Goal:** Can Group DRO make Whisper fairer across accents using automatically discovered speaker groups instead of accent labels, and how close does it come to using the true labels? We will fine-tune Whisper-base four ways: (1) standard fine-tuning (ERM), (2) Group DRO with true first-language labels, (3) Group DRO with k-means clusters of speaker embeddings, and (4) Group DRO with random groups. We expect condition 3 to land between standard fine-tuning and true labels. This isn't guaranteed: Swain et al. (2026) found that Group DRO with true labels did worse than ERM for Whisper on EdAcc, the dataset we use. If that happens here too, our first result is whether Group DRO with true labels beats ERM at all.

**Connection to literature:** We adapt Sohoni et al.'s (2020) "cluster, then Group DRO" method, called GEORGE, from image classification to accented speech. We won't be using GEORGE as-is though. Our features will come from a pretrained speaker-ID model rather than the task model, and we cluster all speakers together because automatic speech recognition (ASR) output has no classes. Sagawa et al. (2020) supply the Group DRO training algorithm and its key requirement: on large networks it only helps with strong regularization. Swain et al. (2026) is the closest work prior to this project: true-label Group DRO on Whisper for L2 speakers. Veliche & Fung (2023) and Dheram et al. (2022) show that k-means clusters of speaker embeddings carry fairness-relevant information in ASR, but they used the clusters as input features or for oversampling, not for Group DRO. Koenecke et al. (2020) motivate the problem, and Radford et al. (2023) describe the model.

**Data:** EdAcc, the Edinburgh International Accents of English Corpus (Sanabria et al., 2023), which Swain et al. also used. It's about 40 hours of conversational English from 122 speakers (59 male, 62 female, 1 other), with self-reported L1 and accent labels. Speakers have a median of about 12 minutes of audio each and no speaker is shared between the released dev and test splits. We pool both splits and re-split by speaker (about 70/15/15), stratified by group and balanced by sex where possible, so test speakers are never heard in training. The labels have a long tail (41 L1 labels and only 17 with at least 3 speakers), so for the true label condition we will clean the labels and merge them into broader groups before training, following Swain et al.'s grouping where possible. We chose EdAcc over L2-ARCTIC because L2-ARCTIC would leave only 12 training speakers to cluster (about 2 per cluster at k = 6), too few for k-means to find accent structure.

**Methods:** To find groups without accent labels, we run each clip through a pretrained speaker-recognition model (ECAPA-TDNN, from SpeechBrain) and average each speaker's embeddings into a single vector. We then reduce these vectors with PCA and cluster the speakers with k-means. We set k to the number of true accent groups so that conditions 2 and 3 use the same number of groups, and we try other values of k. Every condition fine-tunes Whisper-base (74M parameters) with the same hyperparameters. For Group DRO, we use the online algorithm of Sagawa et al., which raises the weight of whichever group currently has the highest loss. A model this size can memorize our roughly 13k training clips, and Sagawa et al. showed that Group DRO then stops helping. We therefore regularize mainly with early stopping on validation worst-group WER, which worked for their transformer (BERT) when strong L2 did not. Strong weight decay is a secondary option. Our baselines are standard fine-tuning (ERM) and zero-shot Whisper-base.

**Evaluation:** We measure word error rate (WER) on the held-out test speakers and report it separately for each true accent group. For each model, we report the average WER, the worst group's WER, and the gap between the best and worst groups. Each condition is run with 3 random seeds, and we report the mean and standard deviation. Many groups have only one or two test speakers, so we also compute confidence intervals over test utterances. To explain why condition 3 does or doesn't work, we compare the clusters with true accent groups and with speaker sex using the adjusted Rand index (ARI) and normalized mutual information (NMI). As sanity checks, zero-shot WER should be imperfect but reasonable, ERM should beat zero-shot, and we will listen to a sample of the clips with the worst transcripts. Our question is answered by comparing condition 3 with conditions 2 and 4. Our main number is how much of the improvement in worst-group WER from ERM to true-label Group DRO the clusters recover (0% means the clusters help no more than ERM and 100% means they match the true labels).

**Compute:** We will train on a gaming laptop with an RTX 3070 GPU (8 GB), with free Google Colab GPUs as a backup and for running some conditions in parallel. The four conditions times three seeds make 12 fine-tuning runs of about an hour or less each, so roughly 12 GPU-hours in total. Computing speaker embeddings and clustering is much cheaper and can run on the same machine. The full dataset is about 7 GB.

**Fallback:** If the clusters turn out to capture something other than accent, such as speaker sex, we will report that as a finding and focus on conditions 1 and 2: does Group DRO with true labels improve accent fairness for small Whisper models on EdAcc? If EdAcc's labels or WER prove too noisy, we will run conditions 1 and 2 on L2-ARCTIC (Zhao et al., 2018), where 24 speakers with 6 different first languages read the same sentences, giving a more controlled test. If Group DRO training is unstable, we will switch to a simpler reweighting baseline that upweights groups by inverse frequency or by their loss.
